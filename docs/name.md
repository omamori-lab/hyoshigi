# The name

hyoshigi is named after the hyōshigi (拍子木), the wooden clappers of the
Japanese night watch, as a metaphor for a monitor that warns before an
agent's action does harm.

- Hyōshigi are square blocks of hard wood struck together, to keep time, to
  give signals in the theatre and on the night watch's rounds
  ([Daijisen, 「拍子木」](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%8B%8D%E5%AD%90%E6%9C%A8-121267)).
- On winter nights the watch walked the neighbourhood striking clappers to
  prevent fires
  ([Daijisen, 「夜回り」](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%9C%E5%9B%9E%E3%82%8A-654930)),
  and 火の用心, "mind the fire", was the call it cried as it walked
  ([Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, 「火の用心」](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%81%AB%E3%81%AE%E7%94%A8%E5%BF%83-612106)).
- In Edo-period towns, the keepers of the ward gates, also called bantarō,
  struck clappers on their night rounds
  ([Oguma, 「火の番」, World Encyclopedia](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%81%AB%E3%81%AE%E7%95%AA-612078)).
- Clappers also signal the start, the breaks and the end of Shinto rites,
  festivals, folk performing arts, sumo, puppet theatre and kabuki
  ([Tomita, 「柝」, World Encyclopedia](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%9F%9D-49797)).
  Their origin is unclear; Miyamoto thinks they probably began as a ritual
  implement for driving off evil spirits with sound
  ([Miyamoto, 「拍子木」, Nipponica](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%8B%8D%E5%AD%90%E6%9C%A8-121267)).

We take the name from the night watch: it struck the clappers on its rounds,
calling 火の用心, so that a fire was prevented before it started. A monitor
does the same for an agent: it watches each proposed action and sounds a
warning, and the control protocol decides what to do. We have no connection
with any shrine, temple or religious practice, and mean the name with deep
respect for the tradition. Corrections are welcome.
